What the tool does

A guide pulse turns the motor at the guide rate. If the gears have slack, the first part of every pulse after a change of direction is spent closing the gap, and the star does not move. The tool pulses in small steps, finds the star after each one, and works out how much commanded time the star's movement does not account for. That missing time is the slack.

slack (ms) = commanded time of the leg − star travel ÷ guide rate

It needs nothing but PHD2. Any mount PHD2 can pulse-guide works — worm gears, spur gears, belts. The example below is a Losmandy G11 declination axis, measured before and after adjusting its spring-loaded worm block on 4 November 2026.

1 · The Measure chart

  1. Horizontal axis — commanded time. Net seconds of guide pulse sent: rightward while pulsing North, back leftward while pulsing South. It is what you asked the mount to do.
  2. Vertical axis — where the star actually went, in arcseconds along the axis being tested. Drift on the other axis is removed automatically.
  3. The dotted diagonal — a perfect mount. No slack: the star would follow the command exactly and the trace would lie on this line going out and coming back.
  4. Leg 1, the preload. Takes up whatever slack is in the train so that leg 2 starts from a known state. Its slope is the real speed once things are moving.
  5. The flat stretch after the first turn — this is the backlash. The command reverses but the star stays put while the motor turns through the gap. Its width, read on the horizontal axis, is the slack in milliseconds.
  6. The sloped part of leg 2 — the gears are engaged again and the star moves at the guide rate. If this slope is clearly shallower than the dotted line, something in the train is springy (see “fitted rate”).
  7. The second turn, back the other way. Same story; the two reversals should agree to within about 10%.
  8. The gap at the end. The command came back to zero but the star did not — it is short by about one slack. A loop that is wide means lots of slack; a loop that collapses onto the diagonal means none.
  9. Before and after. The grey dashed trace is the same test before the adjustment. Its flat stretches are much longer: about 6000 ms then, about 3800 ms after (the two brackets read 4.1 s and 3.5 s).

2 · The result table

columnwhat it means
reversalWhich turn: North→South is leg 2, South→North is leg 3.
slackThe number to track. Milliseconds of pulse lost at that turn, scored at the guide rate you entered. Shown as “≥ whole leg” when the star never got going — then use more steps.
on the skyThe same slack in arcseconds: what a guide correction loses at every reversal.
star movedHow far the star really travelled on that leg.
first clear moveHow long into the leg the star was first clearly moving — a rough cross-check on the slack.
fitted rateThe speed measured once moving (“springiness”). Expect it within about 10% of the guide rate. It is shown, not used: scoring against a fitted rate turned out to swing results by ±10% from run to run.
averageMean of the two reversals. Compare this between adjustments.
effortOnly with a Gemini-II connected: motor duty while moving. If slack goes down but effort climbs, the worm is binding — you have traded backlash for friction and a stall risk.

3 · The Live adjust chart

  1. Each dot is one reversal, plotted at the moment it finished. Orange: the turn going North→South; blue: South→North.
  2. The line is the pair average of the last two reversals. It cancels slow drift along the axis (polar alignment), which makes one direction read high and the other low. Watch the line, not single dots.
  3. An adjustment. You turned a screw here. Within one or two reversals the dots settle at a new level.
  4. A star jump. Someone touched the mount; that leg is not scored, and the run carries on.
  5. Effort bars (Gemini-II only) under each reversal. A sudden rise is the warning to back off.

Why not just watch PHD2's guide graph?

It seems as if you could adjust while guiding and watch the declination line. In practice the graph cannot show this:

  • Guiding hides the slack. PHD2 is busy correcting; a reversal shows up as a small overshoot mixed with seeing, not as a number of milliseconds.
  • Reversals are rare. A well-aligned mount may reverse declination a few times an hour, so you would wait a long time between readings. Live adjust forces a reversal every 20–30 seconds.
  • Everything is mixed together — seeing, drift, periodic error, dithers, backlash compensation. The tool moves one axis on purpose and measures only that.

A good adjusting session

  1. Run Measure once to get a starting number. Note the pose (where the scope points) — keep it the same.
  2. Switch to Live adjust. Make one small change at a time (a fraction of a turn), then wait for two or three reversals.
  3. Watch effort, if you have it, and listen to the mount. Smaller slack with rising effort is a worse mount, not a better one.
  4. When the pair average stops improving, back off slightly and run Measure again to record the final number.
  5. Check cold: gear trains tighten as they cool. Adjusting warm and running cold is a classic way to get stalls.
  6. Then let your guiding software set its backlash compensation from its own test (PHD2 Guiding Assistant).

Run-to-run spread is about ±10% even when nothing changed. Do not chase a 5% difference; repeat the run.

About

Backlash Tool . Runs on this computer only (it listens on 127.0.0.1) and talks to PHD2's server on port 4400. It sends guide pulses and nothing else; it refuses to run while PHD2 is guiding.

Every run is saved as a CSV in .

Settings for unusual setups can be given as environment variables: PHD2_HOST, PHD2_PORT, BACKLASH_PORT, BACKLASH_DATA.

Optional: Losmandy Gemini-II motor effort

Reads the controller's own Servo feed (/motor.cgx, read only) to show motor duty beside each reversal as a friction warning. Kept in memory only; nothing is saved.
